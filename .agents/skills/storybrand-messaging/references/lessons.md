# Ten supporting lessons

Original practical summaries, not transcripts or quotations. These are writing heuristics to test with an audience, not proven laws of persuasion. The source links identify the teaching behind each topic; the cautions are editorial safeguards for this kit.

## 1. Frame a frustration fairly

Use when a customer feels an unnecessary burden. Name the inconvenience in ordinary words, then explain what the offer changes. A “shouldn't have to” opening can express empathy. Do not follow it with an absolute “won't” unless the business can actually support that promise.

Deliver: one empathetic line, one bounded benefit, one claim to verify.

Source: [Two Words That Will Grow Your Business](https://youtu.be/oHkt8UVNBTQ).

## 2. Own a specific problem

Use when the positioning could describe almost any business. Ask when and where the customer notices the need. Replace a category such as “productivity problems” with an observable moment such as searching several calendars before confirming a booking. Pick one audience and one problem, without inventing customer research.

Deliver: broad version, specific version, question to test with customers.

Source: [How To Grow Your Business With Storytelling](https://youtu.be/C9CBjaAOuNE).

## 3. Build PEACE soundbites

Use when several channels need a consistent message. Write one sentence each for Problem, Empathy, Answer, Change, and End Result. Keep the answer concrete and the change aspirational. Empathy need not claim that the founder personally experienced the problem. Keep the core meaning consistent while adapting length to the channel.

Deliver: five labeled lines and a suggested placement for each.

Source: [How To Sell Anything With The StoryBrand Soundbite Strategy](https://youtu.be/5OJT1ph-yL4).

## 4. Close with fit and a next step

Use when a buyer understands the offer but needs a decision path. Restate who it suits and why, address the actual objection with facts, then name the next action. An assertive close cannot resolve an unsupported claim or hide poor fit. Avoid claiming that a purchase is universally the right decision.

Deliver: fit statement, evidence or unanswered objection, clear CTA.

Source: [How to write an Elevator Pitch that actually works](https://youtu.be/L5m4nFtzgbs).

## 5. Explain consequences without fear inflation

Use when a longer pitch explains a meaningful problem. Connect the immediate issue to its downstream consequence and, if relevant, a longer-term cost. Use supplied numbers with their scope or leave quantities unknown. Stop when the buyer has enough context; three negative statements are not mandatory.

Deliver: short problem chain followed by the mechanism that addresses it.

Source: [Why Telling Your Story Will Not Grow Your Business](https://youtu.be/2VidhUB66R4).

## 6. Put the buyer first in a deck

Use when a presentation opens with biography and company history. Reorder it around the buyer's situation, material consequences, solution, relevant proof, and next step. Move secondary background to an appendix, but keep evidence needed for trust in the main story. Investor decks still need market, business model, and financial substance.

Deliver: slide outline, speaker notes, list of moved background slides.

Source: [Why Telling Your Story Will Not Grow Your Business](https://youtu.be/2VidhUB66R4).

## 7. Draft a short video arc

Use for an opening video or ad. Outline the problem, its consequence, the approach, and a plausible result. A rough 0–4, 4–7, 7–12, 12–21 second storyboard is a starting constraint, not a guaranteed attention formula. Read aloud and time the actual recording. Never invent a customer testimonial or imply accelerated results without evidence.

Deliver: timed storyboard, spoken script, CTA, and a note that timings need rehearsal.

Source: [The 21-Second Hook Formula](https://youtu.be/OJvMponNibU).

## 8. Make the first impression understandable

Use for headlines, bios, and confusing opening paragraphs. Identify unclear nouns, jargon, and missing context. Replace them with what is offered, who it serves, and why it matters. Ask a target reader to explain the offer and next step back to you. “Zero cognitive load” is shorthand for clarity, not a measurable score supplied by this kit.

Deliver: original, rewrite, and comprehension-test questions.

Source: [Why Cognitive Load is Holding Back Your Business](https://youtu.be/iG7qdXM9l_4).

## 9. Give a problem-led elevator pitch

Use for a spoken introduction that drifts into a feature list. Start with the buyer's problem, express empathy, add verified authority if available, introduce the product, explain one relevant mechanism, describe a bounded result, and invite the next step. Omit authority rather than fabricate it. Shorten for the actual time available.

Deliver: spoken pitch and a shorter version; rehearse before claiming a duration.

Source: [How to write an Elevator Pitch that actually works](https://youtu.be/L5m4nFtzgbs).

## 10. Prompt in stages, then edit

Use when AI-generated copy is generic. Supply facts first; work through problem, emotional effect, specific need, product mechanism, realistic stakes, CTA, and desired outcome. Review each stage against the brief before combining. The structure is optional: a short product page may need the offer immediately, not several paragraphs of agitation.

Deliver: assembled copy, shorter channel version, and a list of assumptions and evidence gaps.

Source: [How to Use AI and Story to Grow Your Business](https://youtu.be/P9wdRb88qU8).
